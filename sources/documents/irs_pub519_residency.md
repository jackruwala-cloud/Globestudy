# Residency for Tax Purposes: Substantial Presence Test and the Exempt Individual Rule (Pub. 519)

## Two kinds of alien for tax purposes
For U.S. tax purposes, a noncitizen ("alien") is either a nonresident alien or a resident alien. Resident aliens are generally taxed on worldwide income like U.S. citizens; nonresident aliens are generally taxed only on U.S.-source income and file Form 1040-NR. You are a resident alien if you meet either the Green Card Test or the Substantial Presence Test for the calendar year.

## The Substantial Presence Test
You meet the Substantial Presence Test if you were physically present in the United States for at least 31 days during the current year AND 183 days during the 3-year period counting: all days in the current year, 1/3 of the days in the first preceding year, and 1/6 of the days in the second preceding year.

## Exempt individual: students do not count days for up to 5 years
Days you are present as an "exempt individual" do NOT count toward the Substantial Presence Test. A student temporarily present on an F, J, M, or Q visa is an exempt individual. A student is generally treated as an exempt individual for 5 calendar years (these do not have to be consecutive). Because these days are not counted, most F-1 students remain nonresident aliens for their first 5 calendar years in the United States. A teacher or trainee on a J or Q visa follows different (2-year) exempt-individual rules.

## Why this matters
Your residency status determines which tax form you file (Form 1040-NR for nonresident aliens vs. Form 1040 for resident aliens), whether your worldwide income is taxable, whether you owe FICA taxes on wages, and which deductions, credits, and tax treaty benefits you can claim. Determining residency status is a high-stakes tax question; confirm your specific facts with a qualified tax professional or your school's international student office.
