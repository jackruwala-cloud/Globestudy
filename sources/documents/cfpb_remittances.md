# Sending Money Abroad: Remittance Transfer Protections (CFPB)

## What a remittance transfer is
A remittance transfer is generally an electronic transfer of money that a consumer in the United States sends to a person or business in another country, through a remittance transfer provider (for example a bank, credit union, or money transfer company). Federal consumer-protection rules give senders specific rights when the transfer is above a small-dollar threshold and is sent by a provider that handles remittance transfers in the normal course of business.

## Your rights before you pay (disclosures)
Under the federal remittance rule, providers must generally give you a prepayment disclosure and a receipt showing key information before you pay: the exchange rate, certain fees and taxes, and the total amount of money expected to be delivered to the recipient (the amount the recipient will get, not counting certain foreign taxes or recipient-institution fees the provider cannot know). This lets you compare the true cost across providers, because the "cheapest" fee is not always the best exchange rate.

## Cancellation and error-resolution rights
You generally have at least 30 minutes (and sometimes more) after paying to cancel a remittance transfer for a full refund, as long as the money has not already been picked up or deposited. Providers must also give you a way to report problems: if the money did not arrive or an error occurred, you generally have 180 days to notify the provider, and the provider must investigate and, where appropriate, refund or resend.

## Practical tips for students
Compare the total cost (fees plus exchange-rate margin), keep your receipt with the confirmation code, and use the disclosed exchange rate to check how much your family will actually receive. These are general consumer-finance protections, not tax or immigration advice, and do not change any reporting obligations you may have.
